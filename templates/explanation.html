Experiment no. 03

Lee’s method, made intelligible.

Determine the thermal conductivity of a bad conductor by balancing the heat crossing the specimen against the rate at which a metal disc loses heat.

AimPrincipleTheoryWorking equationSymbols & SI units

Aim

To find the coefficient of thermal conductivity of a bad conductor, such as ebonite or cardboard, by Lee’s disc method.

What the experiment compares

A poor conductor is placed between a steam chamber and a metallic Lee’s disc. Steam keeps the upper surface hot, while the disc becomes warm. Once both thermometer readings stop changing, the system is at steady state: each second, the heat conducted through the sample equals the heat the metal disc would lose per second at its observed temperature.

Key insight: We do not need to directly measure heat flow through the sample. We obtain it from the disc’s cooling curve after the steam chamber is removed.

Why heat flows

Conduction occurs when there is a temperature gradient. Energy passes from particles in the hotter region, with greater average molecular energy, to particles in the colder region. The transfer continues until thermal equilibrium is approached.

Thermal conductivity, k, measures a material’s ability to conduct heat. Its SI unit is W m−1 K−1. A small value of k means the material resists heat transfer and is a good insulator.

Deriving the working equation

At steady state, heat conducted through the specimen per second is:

Q/t = kA(T₁ − T₂)/x
T₁ is the steam-side temperature, T₂ is the Lee’s-disc temperature, A is sample area, and x is sample thickness.

During cooling, the same disc loses heat per second at temperature T₂:

Q/t = ms |dT/dt|
m is mass of the metal disc and s is its specific heat capacity.

Equating the two gives:

k = [msx / A(T₁ − T₂)] × |dT/dt|
The cooling slope must be evaluated at the steady Lee’s-disc temperature T₂.
